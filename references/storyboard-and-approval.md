# Storyboard and approval

## Write for decisions, not decoration

A storyboard is a production contract. It must let the user judge the film before expensive work begins.

For every shot, state:

- what the viewer learns, feels, or questions;
- what appears and what changes during the shot;
- which line, sound, or authentic clip is heard;
- which evidence supports it;
- which renderer or edit technique earns its place;
- how the shot enters and leaves;
- what assets are required and how many candidates will be prepared;
- any legal, factual, identity, or continuity risk.

Do not describe every shot with the same verbs. “Slow push-in,” “particles gather,” “text fades,” and “card rotates” are symptoms of an un-authored visual system when repeated by default.

## Review package

Before asking for approval, present:

1. title, thesis, audience, and target runtime;
2. full narration or dialogue in order;
3. a compact shot table with timings and sound;
4. renderer mix and why it fits the evidence;
5. authentic clip list with source and verification status;
6. asset-generation budget and continuity families;
7. voice and music plan;
8. unresolved factual or production risks.

Ask for one decision: approve, approve with named changes, or revise. Do not bury the checkpoint beneath implementation detail.

## Approval semantics

- `user-confirmed`: the user approved the presented storyboard.
- `delegated`: before the checkpoint, the user explicitly asked the Agent to make the decision and continue without pausing.
- silence, a previous unrelated approval, or enthusiasm about the concept is not approval.

After approval, lock narration intent and shot order. Small crop, timing, or transition adjustments do not require reconfirmation. Reconfirm when changing the thesis, source claims, quoted material, runtime category, renderer strategy, or substantial narration.

## Timing discipline

Estimate with spoken tests when possible. A Chinese documentary voice often varies widely by punctuation, numbers, English names, and performance; never derive final timings from character count alone.

Mark each shot's internal beats. A 10-second shot may contain four meaningful changes and remain alive; a 4-second frame can feel stalled when nothing changes. Treat visible change and information change separately.
